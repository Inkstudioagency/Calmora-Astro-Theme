From the doorway they look identical: dim room, people on the floor, nobody moving. Then you take both and discover they are almost opposites. The confusion is understandable and worth clearing up, because picking the wrong one is a good way to conclude that slow classes are not for you.

## Yin: mild stress, held long

Yin uses shapes that place gentle, sustained tension on connective tissue, held for three to five minutes. There is sensation. Sometimes quite a lot of it. Props support the shape but do not remove the load, because the load is the point: fascia and joint capsule adapt to long, moderate stress in a way muscle does not.

## Restorative: no stress at all

Restorative removes load entirely. Every shape is built with bolsters and blankets so completely that nothing has to work, and holds run from five to fifteen minutes. If you feel a stretch in a restorative class, the set-up needs another blanket. The aim is not tissue change but a shift in the nervous system.

## How to choose

- Stiff hips, tight hamstrings, a body that feels stuck: yin.
- Exhausted, wired, sleeping badly: restorative.
- Recovering from injury or illness: restorative first.
- Training hard elsewhere and needing range: yin.
- Genuinely unsure: restorative, and ask afterwards.

## Practical notes

Both classes cool you down, so both need layers and socks. Yin is fine to take after a strong practice; restorative is better on its own, or at the end of a long day. Neither should hurt, and in both cases coming out slowly matters more than getting in deeply.

If you have written off slow classes after one bad experience, it is worth checking which one you took. Plenty of people who dislike yin love restorative, and a fair number the other way round.
